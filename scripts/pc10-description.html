What makes a shop cabin a shop, and not a cabin you sell from

Most portable cabins in India are one product with several labels. The same box is offered as a site office, a store, a guard room and a shop, and the only thing that changes is the description. This page is about the version where the building itself is different.

The difference starts at the wall your customer meets. A retail unit is planned around a large service opening: aluminium glazing with a lockable counter opening, sized and positioned for how you actually serve people. That opening is framed and weather-sealed into the chassis while the frame is being fabricated, not cut into a finished cabin afterwards. Around it sit the things a trading unit needs and an office does not: a staff or customer door chosen for your trading pattern, a lockable service shutter that closes the unit overnight, an interior specified as a customer-facing finish, and power points positioned for signage and equipment rather than for desks.

Everything below follows from that. The structure, the panels and the electrical work are the same disciplined build as the rest of the range. What changes is the front, the security, the finish and the layout, and those four things are what you are paying the difference for.

The frontage decision

This is the decision that most affects how the unit trades, and it is the one no competitor in this market publishes in any detail.

Start with how the transaction happens. If your customer stays outside and you serve through an opening, the counter opening is the product: its width, its sill height, how it locks, and whether the glazing beside it is there to display stock or simply to let light in. If your customer comes inside, the door becomes the primary opening and the glazing becomes a shopfront rather than a service point. Those are different buildings with different opening schedules, and choosing between them at enquiry is far cheaper than changing it later.

Then consider closing time. A shutter is not an accessory bolted on at the end. Its fixing points, its housing and the reinforcement around the opening are part of the opening design, which is why the specification treats the door and shutter as one assembly. A unit that is secured properly at night is a unit whose opening was planned to be secured.

Then the signage and power. Retail equipment is not office equipment. A chiller, a display fridge, a fryer, a card machine and an illuminated sign each have their own position and their own load, and they are coordinated with the approved electrical drawing rather than added to whatever sockets happen to be there. Tell us what you will plug in, and where, before the drawing is issued.

What the interior is specified for

The interior of a retail cabin takes a different kind of wear from an office, and the specification reflects that.

Interior walls are 8 to 12 mm plywood with laminate, or 6 to 8 mm HPL panels. The ceiling is an 8 mm decorative board or a 4 mm ACP ceiling. Underfoot, an 18 mm Bison or marine-grade floor board carries a 3 to 4 mm LVT or SPC finish, or a 2 mm commercial vinyl. Those are surfaces chosen to be cleaned repeatedly, to take trolley wheels and dropped stock, and to still look like a shop after a year of it. A site office lining would not.

Insulation is 50 mm glass wool in the walls and 75 mm in the roof. That matters more in a shop than in a store room, because a customer-facing unit is usually occupied through the hottest part of the day with an opening that spends a good deal of time open.

What the site has to prepare

This is the section the category leaves out entirely, and it is the one that most often decides whether a delivery goes well.

The base comes first. The unit arrives complete and is set down in position, so the position has to be ready. A level, compacted, load-bearing base is the requirement. It does not have to be a concrete raft in every case, but it does have to be level and it does have to take the load without settling unevenly, because a unit set down out of level will show it at the door and at the shutter before it shows it anywhere else.

Access comes second, and it is worth checking before you order rather than on the morning of delivery. The smaller sizes travel on a 20 ft trailer; the 30x10 and the 40x10 need a 40 ft trailer. What the vehicle needs is a route in, room to turn, and space beside the final position to set the unit down. Overhead clearance matters as much as width. Where a crane or other lifting equipment is needed, that is arranged and quoted for your site rather than assumed.

Services come third. Power to the unit is your connection; the distribution board, protection and internal circuits are ours. Where the layout includes a sink or handwash point, water supply and drainage to the unit are site scope. Confirm what exists at the position before the layout is finalised, because moving a wet point after the floor is laid is not a small change.

Branding, colour and finish

The exterior colour and coating system are project-selected, and the finish schedule confirms substrate preparation, compatibility, touch-up and the maintenance appropriate to the exposure category. In practice that means a franchise or brand colour can be matched, and the way it is applied and maintained is agreed rather than assumed.

Two honest limits belong here. First, colour matching on a coated steel substrate is agreed against the approved finish schedule, not against a printed swatch held up to a wall. Second, coastal, industrial and heavy-traffic roadside positions are different exposure categories, and the maintenance interval that keeps a brand colour looking right is not the same in each. If the unit is going somewhere aggressive, say so at enquiry: a galvanized iron porta cabin may be the better substrate to start that conversation from.

What a portable cabin shop gives you on a leased plot

Most retail positions in India are not owned outright. They are leased, licensed for a season, allotted on a forecourt, or held on a plot the owner may want back at short notice. That is the situation this build is designed around, and it is where a portable cabin shop earns its cost against a permanent structure.

The unit is set down on a prepared level base rather than founded into the ground. Nothing about it is poured. Engineered lifting and handling points and support locations are provided for each transportable module, so the shop can be lifted and relocated later, moved to the approved lifting, support and transport drawing rather than improvised on the day. If the lease ends, the shop leaves with you and the plot goes back as it was.

Two honest qualifications belong with that. First, relocating is a planned operation involving a trailer and, depending on the size and the position, lifting equipment. It is quoted for the move; it is not included in the purchase. Second, a unit that has been set down, connected and traded from for a year is not a piece of furniture. Services are disconnected, the base at the new position is prepared in advance, and the approved drawing governs the lift. Plan the second move when you plan the first and it is a straightforward job. Discover it on the last day of a lease and it is not.

Compliance is yours, and it is worth knowing early

No prefab manufacturer in this market publishes anything about the permissions a portable shop needs, which is odd, because it is one of the first questions a first-time retailer asks.

The short version is that the building being movable does not remove the obligations that come with trading from it. Registration under the relevant state Shops and Establishments Act, any trade licence your municipal body requires, and any permission needed to place a structure on the plot or forecourt you have in mind are all yours to obtain, and they vary by state and by municipality. We supply the unit, its specification and its documentation. We do not obtain permissions, and any supplier who implies otherwise is worth a second question.

Food service deserves its own line, because it is where the boundary of this product sits. If you intend to prepare or serve food, FSSAI registration or licensing is a separate requirement and is entirely yours. More importantly for your specification: food-service exhaust and plumbing equipment is outside this product's baseline unless it is separately specified. What the shop and kiosk build includes is provision - an exhaust or duct provision, a fresh-air opening, a sink or handwash point, water and waste connections and equipment connection points according to your layout. That is provision for equipment, not a fitted commercial kitchen. If your business is a cafe, a restaurant or a food outlet rather than a shop that happens to sell something edible, the container cafe range is built around that duty and is the better place to start.

Where a shop cabin is the wrong answer

Three cases, stated plainly.

If nobody buys anything at the wall, you do not need this build. A cabin for staff, storage, supervision or site administration is a simpler and cheaper product, and the retail frontage package is money spent on an opening you will never serve through.

If your business is food preparation at any scale, the point above applies. Provision is not a kitchen.

If staff will spend a full working day in the unit and there is no toilet within reach of the position, sanitation is not a detail to solve afterwards. A porta cabin with toilet puts a wet area inside the same footprint and details it properly, and on a retail plot with no facilities that is often the more useful configuration than a slightly larger selling floor. The full porta cabin range sets the options out against each other.

What we need to quote

A shop is quoted from its frontage, not from its floor area, so the useful enquiry looks different from an office cabin enquiry:

  • The size you want, and the plot or frontage width you have to fit it into
  • How you serve: through a counter opening, or across a floor customers walk onto
  • Your closing arrangement: shutter, door, or both, and what has to be secured overnight
  • The opening schedule, with positions: glazing, counter, doors, louvers and any service penetration
  • What you will plug in and where, including chillers, display units, signage and any equipment load
  • Whether sink, handwash or plumbing is needed, and what water and drainage exists at the position
  • Your brand colour or finish requirement, and the exposure at the position
  • The base you intend to set the unit on, who is preparing it, and the delivery access route

The first two change the quotation most. Tell us those early.

Questions shop owners ask

Can I put a portable cabin shop on leased land without a permanent foundation?

The unit is set down on a prepared level base rather than built into the ground, which is exactly why it suits leased and temporary positions, and it carries engineered lifting points so it can be moved again to the approved drawing. Whether your lease and your local authority permit a structure on that plot is a separate question, and it is yours to confirm. We can tell you what base the unit needs; we cannot tell you what your landlord or municipality will allow.

Can you match my franchise colour exactly?

Colour and coating are project-selected and agreed in the approved finish schedule, which is where the match is confirmed against the actual substrate and coating system rather than against a printed swatch. Bring the brand specification to the enquiry and it is agreed before fabrication, not after.

How is it secured at night?

Through the lockable service counter or shutter and the door lockset, both part of the opening assembly rather than added later. Tell us what has to be secured and how the unit is left overnight, and the opening schedule is planned around it.

Can I run a food outlet from it?

You can sell food from a shop. Preparing food is a different scope: the baseline carries provision, meaning an exhaust or duct provision, a fresh-air opening and sink, water, waste and equipment connection points, but not fitted food-service equipment unless it is separately specified. FSSAI registration is yours. If food is the business rather than part of the stock, look at the container cafe range instead.

How is this different from your office cabins?

The frontage, the security, the finish and the layout. The structure and the services discipline are shared with the rest of the range, which is deliberate; a shop should not be a weaker building than an office, only a differently planned one.

What arrives, and what do I still have to buy?

The building arrives complete to the specification above, with its openings, finishes and electrical installation. Shelving, counters, chillers, display units and everything else that makes it your shop is fit-out, and it is quoted separately.
